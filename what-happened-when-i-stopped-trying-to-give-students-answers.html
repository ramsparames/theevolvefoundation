What Happened When I Stopped Trying to Give Students Answers

After the first Launchpad cohort, I came away with a stronger belief that young people often need less advice and more space to think for themselves.

By Rams Parameswaran, Founder 20 November 2026 · 3 min read

When I started Launchpad, I knew I did not want it to be another programme where I stood in front of students and told them what they should do.

That sounds obvious, but it is actually quite difficult.

When a student asks, “What should I do next?”, the natural instinct is to help them find an answer. After all, I have spent many years working, hiring people, coaching teams and making career decisions myself. I have experience that I can share.

But I have also learnt that my answer is not necessarily the right answer for someone else.

So during the first Launchpad cohort, I tried something different.

I asked more questions.

I gave students time to think.

And sometimes, I resisted the temptation to tell them what I thought they should do.

At first, the questions were about answers

Students came into Launchpad with very different concerns.

Some were thinking about careers. Some were unsure about what they were good at. Some had several possibilities in mind and did not know which one to pursue.

Quite naturally, many of the questions sounded like this:

What should I choose?

Which career is right for me?

Am I good enough?

What should I do next?

These are understandable questions. But I started noticing something as the weeks went by.

The questions became more interesting

The more space students had to think, the questions began to change.

Why do I want this?

What do I actually enjoy doing?

What evidence do I have that I am good at it?

What could I try before making a bigger decision?

Am I choosing this because I want it, or because it is what everyone expects of me?

Those are very different questions.

And I think that is where something important happened.

The students did not necessarily become completely certain about their future. I don't think that is realistic after eight weeks.

But they became more comfortable with the process of figuring things out.

What I came away with

Clarity does not always come from finding the answer.

One of the things I have learnt through my own career is that very few important decisions come with complete information.

We make a choice. We learn something. We adjust. We make another choice.

I wanted Launchpad to help students experience that process early.

Looking back at the first cohort, what stayed with me most was seeing students become more comfortable thinking for themselves.

I did not give them a career answer.

I did not tell them what they should become.

What I could do was create a space where they could pause, reflect, question some of their assumptions and make a decision they felt they could own.

That, to me, was more valuable.

Perhaps that is what young people need more of

We spend a lot of time telling young people to make the right choice.

Choose the right course.

Choose the right career.

Build the right skills.

Make the right decision.

But perhaps we should also teach them how to make decisions when there is no obvious right answer.

That is a skill they will use long after college.

The first Launchpad cohort reinforced something I have believed for a long time:

My role is not to give young people answers. It is to help them become better at finding their own.

And sometimes, the best thing I can do is resist the urge to answer.
