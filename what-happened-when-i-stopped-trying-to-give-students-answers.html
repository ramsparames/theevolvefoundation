Clarity · Asking better questions

What Happened When I Stopped Trying to Give Students Answers

A reflection after the first Launchpad cohort on what happens when young people are given more room to think and less pressure to have the right answer.

By Rams Parameswaran, Founder20 November 2026 · 4 min read
THE IDEA

When I started Launchpad, I was quite clear about what I did not want it to become. I did not want to stand in front of a group of students for eight weeks and tell them what they should study, which career they should choose or what they ought to do next.

That sounds straightforward, but in practice it is not always easy. When a young person asks me, “What should I do next?”, my instinct is to help. I have been working for nearly three decades, I have made plenty of career decisions myself, and I have spent a large part of my career hiring and mentoring people. There is a natural temptation to use that experience to give them an answer.

But the more I thought about it, the more I felt that giving an answer was sometimes doing them a disservice. My answer would be based on my experience, my circumstances and what I value. They need to learn how to make decisions based on their own.

So during the first Launchpad cohort, I deliberately tried to do something different. I asked more questions. I gave students time to think. And, perhaps most importantly, I became more comfortable with not immediately filling every silence with advice.

At first, the questions were about finding the right answer

The students came into Launchpad with very different concerns. Some were trying to understand what they were good at. Some were thinking about careers. Some had several possibilities in front of them and were struggling to decide which direction to take.

Many of the early conversations naturally came back to questions such as, “What should I choose?”, “Which career is right for me?” and “What should I do next?” There is nothing wrong with those questions. In fact, they are probably exactly the questions I would have asked at that stage of my own life.

What interested me was what happened when we did not rush to answer them.

Over time, the questions started to change

As the weeks went by, I noticed that the conversations became less about finding a single correct answer and more about understanding the person making the decision. Students started asking themselves why they were attracted to a particular path, what they actually enjoyed doing, what evidence they had about their strengths and what they could try before making a bigger commitment.

They were also beginning to question some of the assumptions they had brought with them. Was this something they genuinely wanted, or simply something they thought they were expected to want? Were they ruling out an option because it was not right for them, or because they had never really explored it?

Those are much more useful questions when you are trying to make a decision about your future.

I don't think the students finished eight weeks later with everything figured out. Nor should they have. At that age, with so much of life still ahead of them, certainty would probably be the more surprising outcome.

What I did see was a greater comfort with figuring things out. That, to me, was a more meaningful outcome.

Clarity does not always come from finding the answer. Sometimes it comes from getting better at asking the question.

One of the things my own career has taught me is that very few important decisions come with complete information. We make a choice, learn something from it, adjust and make the next choice. That is how most careers actually unfold.

I wanted Launchpad to give students a chance to experience that process early, rather than making them believe they needed to have their entire future mapped out before they could take the next step.

Looking back at the first cohort, what stayed with me most was seeing students become more comfortable thinking for themselves. I did not give them a career answer. I did not tell them what they should become. What I could do was create enough space for them to pause, reflect, question some of their assumptions and make a decision they felt they could own.

That, to me, is a much more useful skill than simply knowing what someone else thinks they should do.

Perhaps this is what we need to teach more deliberately

We spend a lot of time telling young people to make the right choice. Choose the right course. Choose the right career. Build the right skills. Make the right decision.

Of course we want young people to make good choices. But life does not give us a set of multiple-choice questions with the correct answer marked at the bottom of the page. There will be decisions where the information is incomplete, where two options could both work and where the only way to learn is to try something.

I think we should teach young people how to be comfortable with that uncertainty. How to gather enough information, make a thoughtful call, learn from what happens and then adjust when necessary.

The first Launchpad cohort reinforced something I have believed for a long time: my role is not to give young people answers. It is to help them become better at finding their own.

And sometimes, the best thing I can do is resist the urge to answer.

Launchpad

Launchpad is an 8-week live, online programme for college students. It creates structured space for students to understand themselves better, explore direction and take ownership of what comes next.

Rams Parameswaran is the Founder of The Evolve Foundation. For nearly 30 years, he built and led technology organisations across the United States and India, hiring, coaching and mentoring professionals along the way.
