We ask students to decide before we ask them to understand themselves

We often ask young people to decide what comes next before giving them much time to understand themselves.

By Rams Parameswaran, Founder 2 October 2026 · 3 min read

We often ask young people what they want to do next before giving them much time to understand themselves.

“What are you going to do next?”

It sounds like a simple question. For a student, it can carry quite a bit of pressure.

Which course should I take? Which internship should I apply for? What career should I choose?

There is plenty of advice around these questions. Build your skills. Get an internship. Prepare for placements. Find your passion.

All of it can be useful. But I think we sometimes skip an important step.

A useful question

Do you understand yourself well enough to make the choice?

Before the answer, there is the understanding.

A student may know they are good at solving problems, explaining things, organising people or building things. They may have interests they keep coming back to.

But strengths and interests do not automatically tell you what career to choose. They give you something to work with.

What comes naturally to me? What holds my attention? What have my experiences taught me about myself? What would I like to explore further?

These questions may not give you a career plan. They can help you see yourself more clearly. And that matters when the next decision comes along.

You do not need to have it all figured out.

I have noticed that students can feel pressure to have everything figured out quite early. I do not think that is necessary.

You do not need to know exactly where you will end up before deciding what to explore next.

A conversation, a small project, an internship or simply trying something new can often teach you more than spending months trying to predict whether something is the right path.

The goal is not to have all the answers. It is to make the next choice with a little more understanding.

This is why we built Student Compass.

Student Compass came from this idea.

It is not a career test, and it does not tell a student what they should become. It is simply a short opportunity to pause and reflect.

In about ten minutes, students think about their strengths, experiences, interests, possibilities and how they approach decisions.

There are no right or wrong answers.

The hope is that they come away noticing something about themselves they had not quite put into words before. Perhaps a strength. An area they want to develop. Or something they would like to explore.

Sometimes the next step becomes clearer when you take a little time to understand where you are starting from.
